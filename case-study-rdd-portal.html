Project overview

What has to happen before a shop can open

Arada builds retail. A tenant signs a lease for an empty shell, then designs and fits out the interior — and every part of that has to be approved by the landlord before the doors can open.

The portal exists because the fit-out process is a supply chain, not a transaction.

A tenant's architect submits drawings. Arada's Retail Design & Delivery team reviews them against the mall's design guidelines. Their MEP engineers separately review the mechanical, electrical and plumbing design because a restaurant's extraction and a clothing store's power load are landlord problems as much as tenant ones. Samples get approved. Site mobilisation gets a certificate. Civil Defence and the electricity authority need letters from the landlord before they will deal with the tenant at all.

Then the fit-out happens, gets inspected, gets snagged, gets re-inspected, and finally — if the dates in the lease were met, and if they weren't, once the penalty is settled — the tenant is given Permission to Trade.

That's thirteen sequential gates involving four different parties, each of whom needs to see a different slice of the same record. It is a genuinely hard information design problem, and it was being run on email.

Why it needed a platform

  • The process is strictly sequential — stage nine can't start until stage eight is signed.
  • Every stage produces documents, and later stages depend on earlier ones.
  • One party is external. Tenants aren't Arada staff and can't be given internal systems.
  • Some outputs are legally operative — statutory NOCs, penalty settlements, handover acceptance.
  • Dates set at lease signing determine money owed thirteen stages later.

Any one of those is manageable in a mailbox. All five together are not.

The challenge

Thirteen gates, four inboxes

The account of the previous process is the team's own. What follows is their list, grouped into the three failures it actually describes.

The record had no home

Information and documents moved across multiple channels, with no centralised view of a tenant or a project. A drawing approved in March lived in whichever mailbox happened to receive it, and the person who needed it in June had to know who to ask.

Nobody could see anyone else's progress

Four teams worked the same file with limited visibility into each other. RDD couldn't tell whether MEP had finished reviewing. The tenant couldn't tell whether anything had happened at all. So everyone compensated the same way — manual follow-up — which is why the brief lists repetitive communication between all four groups as its own separate problem.

Status was a question, not a fact

Tracking the state of requests, approvals and submissions was difficult, and pending actions were difficult to identify. In a strictly sequential process that is the most expensive failure of all: if nobody can see which gate is open, the whole chain waits.

The consequences the team names — delays, missed updates, communication gaps — aren't separate problems. They're what those three failures produce.

Heavy dependency on emails and manual communication, with no single platform where stakeholders could easily access the information relevant to them.

Worth being precise about the stake. A retail lease sets a trade start date, and a late opening penalty per day if the tenant misses it. A process that loses a week of review time doesn't just annoy people — it moves money between the landlord and the tenant, and it does so in a way that neither party can audit. That's what makes this a design problem about evidence rather than convenience.

The users

Four groups, one record

Three of these work for Arada. One doesn't — and that asymmetry shapes most of the design decisions that follow.

Internal · originates

Lease

Signs the tenant and sets the commercial terms. Creates the two documents everything else derives from: New lease (PTL) — the confirmed offer — and Lease executed (Form 01).

Their form captures the areas, the contacts, the fit-out dates and the late opening penalty. Every number the process later argues about is entered here, once.

Internal · owns the process

Retail Design & Delivery

The landlord's design authority. Reviews conceptual and architectural submissions against mall guidelines, approves samples, issues the critical path, runs inspections, raises snag lists and issues the NOCs.

They hold the most stages of any group, and they're the only party who can grant Permission to Trade.

External · submits

Tenant

Not an Arada employee. Appoints a designer and a main contractor, then submits everything: appointments, conceptual design, architectural and MEP packages, samples, pre-mobilisation, inspection requests and close-out.

Their view is the same record with the submit side turned on and the approve side removed — plus a Cancelled and an Acknowledgement queue the internal teams don't have.

Internal · technical gate

MEP

Mechanical, electrical and plumbing engineers. Reviews the MEP design package, approves MEP samples, runs its own inspection checklist and its own close-out, and handles service connections.

MEP runs in parallel with RDD's architectural review, which is why the workflow has two separate close-outs — one RDD, one MEP — before Permission to Trade.

◆

All four dashboards are the same layout — a completion gauge, four count tiles, one recent table. What changes is the table's columns and the sidebar's queues. Building four visually distinct dashboards would have been easier to justify in a design review and much worse in practice: the four groups have to talk to each other about the same record, and a shared mental model is worth more than a tailored one.

Design process

How the structure was arrived at

A note on evidence first: the exported file behind this case study contains final UI. The discovery work is described here rather than shown, and the two diagrams below are drawn from the structure the shipped screens actually implement.

01Requirements came from the process owners, not from users in the abstract

The fit-out sequence isn't a design opinion — it's a contractual and regulatory procedure that RDD already ran. The first job was to write it down precisely: every stage, what document it needs, who submits it, who approves it, and what it unblocks. That inventory is what the thirteen-stage rail is built from.

02Mapping the existing workflow exposed the parallel branch

Laid out linearly, one thing became obvious: MEP review doesn't sit inside RDD's review, it runs alongside it. That single observation shaped the whole structure — it's why the workflow has separate Close-out (RDD) and Close-out (MEP) gates rather than one, and why MEP gets its own dashboard rather than being a section of RDD's.

03The pain points clustered around visibility, not capability

Nobody was missing a feature. Every group could already do their job — they just couldn't see whether anyone else had done theirs. That reframed the design target from build the forms to make the state of the sequence impossible to misread, which is why the stage rail sits at the top of every single record screen rather than on a status page of its own.

04Information architecture: navigate by status, drill by stage

The sidebar is organised by workflow state — New, In progress, Approved, Completed, Returned, Submitted — not by feature or document type. It answers "what needs me?" That's the outer loop. The inner loop is the stage rail inside one tenant: same thirteen chips for everybody, so a conversation between two teams can refer to a stage by name and mean the same thing.

05The one screen worth prototyping properly was the review

Most of this portal is structured forms, which are a solved problem. The drawing review is not: it has to show a CAD drawing at a readable size, collect a comment and a reason, take an attachment, and capture a three-way decision — all without leaving the record. That's the screen where the design work actually happened, and it's covered in its own section below.

Navigation by state, not by feature

The decision behind the sidebar, stated as the comparison it was chosen over.

A feature-based sidebar describes the system. A state-based sidebar describes the user's obligations — and in a sequential approval process, that's the only question anybody opens the tool to answer. The counts beside each queue do the rest: Returned 10 is a to-do list without needing to be called one.

Key features — 01. The shell

The two things on every record screen

Whatever stage you're looking at, and whichever of the four roles you hold, two elements never move.

A persistent tenant header

Tenant name, designation, unit number, unit name, unit type, company, telephone, mobile and email — pinned above every stage form.

In a mall with hundreds of near-identical unit codes, and a reviewer working through a queue of them, losing track of whose record you're in is the failure mode that matters. It costs one card of vertical space on every screen and removes an entire class of error.

The stage rail

Directly beneath it, the thirteen gates as chips — green with a tick for complete, dark for the one you're in, plain outline for stages not yet reached.

Three states, readable in a glance, and identical for all four groups. It's the single element that answers the question the old process couldn't: where is this?

It also does something subtler. Because the rail is always visible, a reviewer can see what their approval will unblock — you're not just clearing a form, you're opening the next gate for somebody else.

The thirteen stages, as the product renders them

Reconstructed from the rail on the Permission to Trade screen, where every gate is complete.

Critical path Tenant appointment Conceptual design Arch design MEP design Sample approval Pre-mobilisation NOC Inspection Close-out (RDD) Close-out (MEP) Service connection Permission to Trade

Green with a tick — complete. Solid with a dot — the stage you're in. On records earlier in the process, stages not yet reached appear as plain outlined chips, so the rail shows past, present and future in one line. Longer journeys add stages: the penalty route also carries Snaglist, Pre-ceiling, Final MEP and LOP summary.

Key features — 02. The workflow

Following one shop from lease to open door

Each of these is a real screen from the portal. Together they are one tenant's journey through the sequence.

Stage 01 — Lease sets the terms

The Initial Notification — Confirmed offer (Form PTL) captures tenancy location and areas, tenant contact details, and the key dates from the offer letter: fit-out commencement, concept design submission, detail design submission, trade start — and the late opening penalty per day.

Stages 02–03 — The tenant appoints, then proposes

The tenant nominates their designer and main contractor, then submits a conceptual design. Both are gates: RDD has to accept the appointment before the design is reviewed, because reviewing drawings from an unapproved consultant wastes everyone's time.

Stages 04–05 — Two design reviews, running in parallel

The architectural package goes to RDD. The MEP package goes to MEP. This is the busiest part of the process and the one with the most documents: the architectural submission alone has sixteen attachment slots — five standard requirements and eleven checklist drawings.

Stages 06–08 — Samples, mobilisation, certificates

Physical material samples are approved separately by both RDD and MEP. Then pre-mobilisation clears the contractor to start on site, and the first NOC is issued.

Stages 09–12 — Inspection, snagging, close-out, connection

The contractor requests inspection; RDD and MEP each run their own checklist; defects become a snag list with scheduled dates; both teams close out separately; utilities get connected.

◆

Notice the snag list's three dates sitting together: fit-out completion, projected opening, and opening as per lease. That's not incidental — the gap between the last two is exactly what the penalty calculation is about, and the design puts them side by side at the moment a delay becomes visible rather than saving the reckoning for later.

Key features — 03. The review

The screen the whole portal is really for

Everything else is a form. This is where a professional judgement gets made and recorded — and where the design had to do actual work.

Three outcomes, because two aren't enough

Approve and reject would have been the obvious pair. It would also have been wrong, because the most common real result of a fit-out design review is neither.

No Objection Clean pass. The stage closes, the next one opens.
No Objection with comments Proceed, but fix these. The tenant isn't blocked, and the comments are on the record.
Revise & Resubmit Not acceptable. Back to the tenant with a reason.

That middle option is the one that saves weeks. Under a binary system, a reviewer with three minor comments has to choose between waving through work they have reservations about, or sending the whole package back and costing the tenant a fortnight. The middle button lets the truthful answer also be the fast one.

The colours run green → teal → amber, so severity is legible before the labels are read.

Everything the decision needs, in one view

AThe drawing is the largest object on screen. A reviewer is reading a CAD elevation at 1:20 — if they have to download it to judge it, the portal has failed and the review goes back to email and a desktop PDF reader.
BComments, reason and attachment sit in one rail beside it. Not in a modal on top of a modal. The reviewer's response is composed while looking at the thing being responded to.
CA structured reason as well as free text. The dropdown makes rejections analysable across tenants — which requirement gets missed most is a question the old process could never answer.
DReviewers can attach their own file. The clearest way to say what's wrong with a drawing is often a marked-up version of it, so the response carries an attachment slot too — the channel runs both ways.

The three buttons sit at the bottom, full width, spaced apart. It's a decision with contractual consequences and it should feel like one — no dropdown, no confirm dialog, just three clearly different commitments.

Scope: desktop only. No mobile or tablet layouts were designed. The portal carries desk work — a CAD elevation judged at readable size, a sixteen-slot submission checklist, an inspection form that runs several screens — and it was scoped for the machine that work is actually done on.

Key features — 04. Certificates

The portal writes the letters

A fit-out can't proceed without the landlord formally telling external authorities it has no objection. Those letters are generated here, from data the portal already holds.

Three certificates, one mechanism

A form at the top, a legal letter at the bottom, and no retyping between them

The screen has two halves. Above: structured inputs — plot, floor, connected load, panel and meter references, project, landlord legal entity. Below: the actual letter those values compose, laid out as correspondence with a reference number and a date, ready to send.

It's rendered bilingually, English and Arabic side by side, because that's the form a UAE authority accepts. Not a language toggle — both columns simultaneously, the same decision the Broker App's agency agreement makes for the same reason.

Three variants exist for three recipients: Civil Defence, SEWA — electricity, water and gas — and the shopping mall operator. Same pattern each time.

AShow the letter, don't just promise it. The person clicking Send is putting the landlord's name to a statutory document. They need to read exactly what goes out, on the screen where they authorise it.
BThe technical references are inputs, not prose. Connected load, SMDB, LV panel and meter references are typed into fields and then composed into the letter — so they're queryable data as well as text in a PDF.
CSave and Send are separate. A statutory letter often waits on a number somebody has to go and find. Save keeps a half-finished certificate; only Send commits it.

Key features — 05. The penalty

The most adversarial document in the relationship

And, I'd argue, the best-designed screen in the portal — because it's built to be checked rather than believed.

Late Opening Penalty

A charge that shows its own arithmetic — and its own exceptions

If a tenant opens late, the lease imposes a penalty per day. Calculating it means comparing a programme of milestone dates against what actually happened, then deciding how much of the slippage was the tenant's fault.

The screen does this in six labelled sections, and three details make it remarkable:

  • The fields cite their own sources. Values appear as [Late Opening Penalty from Form 01], [Trade date] minus [Form 01 issue date] — the form tells you where each number came from.
  • The matrix header carries the formula. The delay column is literally labelled Delay days (C−D), and the total reads B−(C+D). You can audit the calculation from the column headings.
  • The tenant's defences are first-class fields. Landlord delay and delay beyond tenant control are separate deduction sections, not notes in a comment box.

Then section F lists eight named reasons for waiver as checkboxes — landlord works, permit delays, force majeure, a first-of-its-kind retail concept, design disagreements with Arada's own guidelines, and more.

Why the waiver list is the whole point

The landlord wrote this form. The landlord collects the penalty. And the landlord has enumerated, on the face of the document, eight specific circumstances in which the tenant shouldn't have to pay — including two that are explicitly the landlord's own fault.

That's a design decision with real teeth. A penalty notice that only computes the charge invites a dispute conducted by email, with the tenant assembling their case from scratch and the landlord assessing it ad hoc. A notice that pre-structures the tenant's defence turns the same conversation into filling in a form both sides already agree on the shape of.

It is, in the most literal sense, designing for fairness — and it's the thing I'd point to if someone asked whether enterprise UX can be more than tidy tables.

The final gate, and its four signatures

Once the penalty is settled, Permission to Trade is the last screen. It carries the tenant's formal request, then a no-objection block signed by four named roles — RDD Senior Manager, RDD Director, Leasing Senior Administration Manager, Leasing Director — and finally a fifth section handing the unit over to Mall Operations.

Five sign-offs across three departments, on one screen, with the penalty summary immediately above them. The design keeps them in one place because that is the moment the whole thirteen-stage record is being certified as complete.

Before / after

Email thread → tenant record

The same thirteen stages, run two ways.

Before — four mailboxes
  • The record was whichever attachments each party happened to keep.
  • Status was established by asking someone, and waiting.
  • Progress of the other three teams was invisible.
  • Follow-up was the only tracking mechanism, so everyone did it constantly.
  • Reviews came back as prose in a reply, with the drawing re-attached.
  • Certificates were retyped into letter templates from data in other emails.
  • Penalties were reconciled after the fact from dates scattered across the thread.
  • Accountability was whoever was last on the CC line.
→
After — one portal
  • The record is one tenant file, with every submission attached to the stage it belongs to.
  • Status is the stage rail: done, current, not yet reached.
  • Progress is shared — all four groups read the same rail.
  • Follow-up is replaced by queues that carry their own counts.
  • Reviews happen on the drawing, with a comment, a reason and one of three decisions.
  • Certificates are composed from portal data into a bilingual letter you read before sending.
  • Penalties derive from dates the portal already holds, with the formula in the column headers.
  • Accountability is the queue a document is sitting in, and whose queue that is.
◆

The pattern across every row is the same substitution: a conversation is replaced by a state. That's the whole thesis of the portal, and it's why the stage rail — not the dashboard, not the forms — is the component the design lives or dies by.

Impact & benefits

What the design does, and what it's expected to improve

Separated deliberately. The first group points at screens in this case study. The second is anticipated but unmeasured.

Evidenced in the design

01

Information is centralised on the tenant record

One file per tenant, carrying the persistent header, the thirteen-stage rail, and every submission, review, certificate and checklist attached to the stage it belongs to.

02

Four groups collaborate through one shared structure

Identical dashboard layout, identical stage rail, role-scoped columns and queues. Two teams discussing a tenant refer to the same named stage and mean the same thing.

03

Status is visible without asking

Three chip states show past, present and future at a glance; the sidebar's state-based queues carry counts, so pending actions announce themselves.

04

Reviews happen in the portal, on the drawing

A full-size drawing viewer with comments, a structured reason, an attachment and three graded outcomes — including the middle one that lets work proceed with conditions recorded.

05

Statutory documents are generated, not retyped

Three bilingual NOC variants composed from structured fields, previewed in full before sending, with Save and Send kept separate.

06

The commercial reckoning is auditable

Penalty fields cite their sources, the matrix headers carry the arithmetic, landlord-caused and out-of-scope delays are deductible, and eight waiver grounds are enumerated on the form.

Anticipated, but not measured

The improvements the team expects are the ones the brief lists: reduced email dependency, faster and more structured communication, better document management, clearer accountability, improved tracking, and a smoother workflow between business, design, tenant and technical groups.

Those follow logically from the mechanisms above. But they are expectations, not findings. I have no before-and-after data on approval cycle times, email volumes, or how often a stage gets returned — and I'm not going to convert design intent into numbers that were never captured.

What would actually prove it

  • Days per stage, and days from lease execution to Permission to Trade.
  • Share of reviews resolved as No Objection with comments rather than Revise & Resubmit.
  • Return rate per stage — which gate sends work back most often, and why.
  • Late opening penalties charged versus waived, and on which of the eight grounds.
  • Email volume between the four groups about a tenant, before and after.

The portal now holds the data for every one of these. That's arguably its most valuable long-term output — a process that previously left no analysable trace.

Where this goes next

Show the programme against the clock

The portal knows every milestone date and every actual date — it computes penalties from them. What it doesn't yet do is show a tenant, mid-process, that they are eleven days behind on the MEP submission and what that will cost. A live view of the critical path against today's date would turn the penalty screen from a reckoning into a warning.

Mark up the drawing itself

Reviewers can attach a marked-up file, which is the pragmatic answer. Annotating directly on the drawing in the viewer — a pin with a comment, anchored to a coordinate — would keep the critique attached to the thing being criticised, and make "which requirement gets missed most" answerable by location as well as by reason.
