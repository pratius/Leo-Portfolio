Case Study · Super app · Concept, not released

Arada
Super App

Find a home, reserve it, pay for it, move in, and run it without leaving one app. Eighty-three screens covering the whole relationship between a developer and the person living in the building.

ClientArada, a Sharjah & Dubai master developer
ProductSuper App (property, payments and services)
My roleUI/UX Designer, screens designed end to end
StatusDesigned, not released
PlatformNative mobile, 428×926 base
UsersBuyers, tenants, owners and residents
ToolsFigma, Photoshop, Illustrator
Scope83 screens: flows, UI, states
Not launched

This app was never released. I designed the screens and the product didn't go to build. So there are no adoption figures on this page, no usage data and no outcomes. What's here is the design decisions and the reasoning behind them. Everything shown comes from the actual Figma screens.

Browse, transact, and then live there. Click any screen on this page to open it full size.

The brief

Four products that normally live in four apps

A developer's relationship with a customer doesn't end at the sale. That's where it begins. The person searching for an apartment becomes the person reserving it, then paying instalments on it, then reporting a broken air conditioner in it. This design puts all four in one place.

Before you own

Explore Property

Buy or lease, across three communities, with an eight-level filter, floor plans, 360° views, site visits and an online reservation.

Money

Payments

Reservation fees, security deposits, service quotations, amenity bookings and annual maintenance contracts. Paid by saved card, wallet or Apple Pay.

Once you're in

Arada Care

Home services, issue reporting, maintenance packages and amenity booking, each scoped to one of your properties.

Paperwork

E-Services

Ten request types: NOC, access cards, work permits, pet registration, move-in, move-out and more. Each has its own form and its own status trail.

The three onboarding slides name the three pillars in order: home, payments, services. The sign-in card offers Continue as guest underneath. You shouldn't need an account to look at apartments.

01 · Explore Property

Buying and leasing run on the same screens

Same journey, different money attached. The screens share a layout and swap the vocabulary. That keeps the build small, and a customer who has done one already knows the other.

Same screen, different tenure. Payment Plan becomes Rental & Charges, Reserve Now becomes Lease Now, and the fee notice changes from a coral AED 10,000 reservation to a blue AED 1,000 deposit.

The filter goes eight levels deep

Community, property type, cluster, project, building, architectural type, unit, bedrooms. That's how a master developer's inventory is actually structured. A buyer who already knows they want a specific building in Aljada shouldn't have to scroll past Masaar to find it.

Two exits from every property

Reserve Now takes a payment. Book a Visit takes a date and a time slot. Both are full-width and stacked. A first-time off-plan buyer and a returning investor want opposite things from the same screen, and neither should have to hunt for theirs.

The fee is stated above the button

A coloured band sits directly above Reserve Now. It reads “You will pay an amount of AED 10,000 online to reserve this property.” Nobody arrives at the payment screen surprised that there's a payment.

Four utilities in a row

Floor Plan, Brochure, 360° View and Map View sit as a row of four icon tiles instead of being buried in the accordions below. A serious buyer opens all four, so all four are above the fold.

02 · The paperwork moment

Documents depend on where you live

A lot of off-plan property is bought from abroad. Someone in London buying in Sharjah has no Emirates ID, so asking for one is a dead end. But asking everyone for a passport wastes the time of the majority, who do have an Emirates ID.

Resident in the UAE 4 inputs
  • Emirates ID, front
  • Emirates ID, back
  • Emirates ID number
  • Expiry date
Buying from abroad 11 inputs
  • Nationality, country of residence
  • Address, city
  • National ID, front and back
  • National ID number and expiry
  • Passport scan
  • Passport number and expiry
Why it matters Nobody blocked
  • The overseas buyer isn't asked for a document they can't have
  • The resident isn't asked for nine fields they don't need
  • One question up front decides the shape of everything below it

03 · Arada Care

Every service starts with the property

A customer can hold more than one property (an apartment in Aljada, a villa in Masaar). So the picker sits at the top of every service screen as a chip with a pencil, and it never scrolls away.

Report it, get quoted, approve or reject, pay. The quotation itemises parts and labour, with tax and the service fee shown separately. The total isn't a number the resident has to take on trust.

You can record the problem instead of describing it

The request form takes a photo and a voice note. Some faults are far easier to say than to write: a noise, an intermittent fault, a room name the resident doesn't know the English word for. The waveform and mic sit inline, at the same weight as the photo upload.

A quotation you can turn down

Reject is the same size as Proceed to Pay, outlined instead of filled. A resident who thinks AED 900 is too much for the job is having a legitimate reaction. Declining shouldn't feel like an error state.

Three tiers, collapsed by default. Buy Now sits on the collapsed card as well as the open one. Someone renewing last year's package shouldn't have to read the inclusions again.

Saved cards still ask for the CVV. The card is stored, the three digits aren't. Two seconds of typing, and it's the difference between a stolen phone being an inconvenience and a spending spree.

04 · Raise an Issue

Three complaint types, three forms

This is the piece of the design I'd defend hardest. Locating a broken light in a shared corridor and reporting a problem in someone else's flat are not the same question, so the app doesn't ask them the same way.

One dropdown at the top rewrites everything below it.

Common area 7 fields
  • Complaint category, sub category
  • Precinct
  • Unit
  • Floor
  • Common area
General area 3 fields
  • Description
  • Photo upload
Other unit 4 fields
  • Complaint category
  • Description
  • Photo upload

Location fields only where they're needed

A fault in a common area has to be found by a technician who wasn't there when it was reported. So the form takes four location fields: precinct, unit, floor and which common area. A general-area problem is visible the moment someone walks past it, so a photo and a sentence are enough.

The status screen reads back what you gave

Whatever the form asked for, the status view lists it back as a labelled record. The resident can check whether they picked the right floor without re-opening the form, and the technician sees the same list. Nothing is collected that isn't shown back.

05 · E-Services

Ten request types on one screen

The property chip, the Submit Request and View Status tabs, a service-type dropdown, and then whatever that request actually needs. The shell never moves. Only the middle changes.

Two fields or ten. The header, the tabs, the dropdown and the submit button are in the same place every time.

Only Move-In is multi-step

Move-In has a four-node rail across the top: resident details, emergency contact, occupancy, vendor. It's the only request that asks for more than twenty inputs, and the only one where a person would reasonably stop halfway and come back.

Everything else is one screen with a Submit button. Breaking a four-field form into steps is just ceremony.

The rule “Should not be a resident of the same unit” sits above the emergency contact fields in red, before anything is typed. A validation that appears after submission would be a wasted round trip. The whole point of an emergency contact is that they're somewhere else.

06 · After you submit

Approvals and repairs track differently

An approval and a repair job feel the same to a resident. You asked for something and now you're waiting. They aren't the same thing, and the trackers say so.

Requests wait for a decision

NOC, access card, work permit, move-in, pet registration.

  1. Received RequestWe received your service request
  2. ReviewYour service request has been reviewed
  3. ApprovedCompleted on a stated date

Services need a technician and a payment

Home services and anything that sends someone out.

  1. Received RequestWe received your service request
  2. AssignedYour service request has been assigned
  3. Quotation ReceivedView and pay for service execution. The pay button lives inside this step
  4. CompletedWith an estimated date while it's still open

The pay button sits inside the tracker

When a quotation arrives, View & Pay appears in the tracker at the quotation node. No footer bar, no notification the resident has to go and find. The thing you have to do sits where it's required.

Completed requests stay in the list

Every status view has All, In Progress and Completed, with the groups as collapsible accordions. Finished requests stay available. The access card you were issued last year is the record you need when you lose it.

Honestly

What I'd fix before this shipped

It never went to build. Nothing here was caught in QA or worn smooth by real use, so what follows is just what I can see in my own file.

Loose ends in the file

  • Two names for one shell. The same request screen is headed “Submit Your Request” in some flows and “E-Services” in others. I need to pick one.
  • Placeholder copy in onboarding. All three slides still have Lorem Ipsum under the headline. The first thing a new user reads is the least finished text in the app.
  • Profile is mostly empty dashes. Nationality, gender, date of birth and address all render as “———”. It needs a real empty state that asks people to fill it in.

Decisions I'd want to test

  • Eight filter levels is right for the inventory and possibly wrong for a first-time buyer. I'd want to watch someone use it before I defend it.
  • Guest browsing has no defined moment where the app asks for an account. It should be the reservation, but I never drew that transition.
  • Ten request types in one dropdown works on paper. At fifteen it won't, and this is a developer that keeps adding services.

No metrics appear anywhere on this page. The app wasn't released, so there's nothing to measure and nothing to claim. What's here is the reasoning. That's the part that transfers to the next project anyway.
